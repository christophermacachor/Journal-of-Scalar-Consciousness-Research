Introduction: The Emergence of a Novel Biological Agent The definition of life has long been under constant debate within the life sciences. The more we push the boundaries of knowledge and explore our planet, the more difficult it becomes to reach a consensus. While some defend the idea that living organisms are those exhibiting basic metabolism, others argue that the ability to reproduce—either independently or by exploiting the metabolism of another life form—is essential. The classification of the simplest life forms remains under debate, with candidates ranked either by cell size or by the number of genes encoded in their DNA. Some define bacteria as the simplest life forms today, with *Carsonella ruddii* often cited as a prime example, possessing a genome of only 182 genes and residing at the frontier between a symbiotic bacterium and an organelle. However, because this symbiont requires a host to survive, other bacterial candidates with larger genomes that also have the ability to grow in laboratory conditions, such as the marine bacterium *Pelagibacter ubique*, with a genome of fewer than 1,400 genes, are also considered. This issue becomes even more complex when considering viruses and viroids as parasitic life forms. These entities are often defined as "molecular complexes" specialized in hijacking host metabolism to replicate themselves. The simplest virus known is the hepatitis Delta virus, with only one open reading frame and a genome of 1.7 kilobases. It is a satellite virus that depends on the co-infection with hepatitis B virus. Recently, the definition of life has been challenged again with the discovery of obelisks. The human gut is a complex ecosystem, a bustling metropolis of microbial life that profoundly influences our physiology and even our mood. The communication pathway between this internal world and our brain, known as the gut-brain axis, is a subject of intense scientific scrutiny. It is within this intricate network that a remarkable discovery was reported in *Cell* in 2024: the Obelisk (Zheludev et al., 2024). This entity is not a virus, nor a viroid, but a novel class of biological agent defined by its unique molecular structure and intriguing quantum properties. The formal definition describes it as a circular, single-stranded RNA molecule, approximately 950–1,050 nucleotides in length. Its discovery in fecal samples through meta-transcriptomic analysis has opened a new frontier in our understanding of the microbiome's complexity. Obelisks reside intracellularly in gut bacteria, primarily within *Streptococcus* and *Faecalibacterium* species, and are remarkably prevalent, detected in ~7% of stool metatranscriptomes and ~50% of oral metatranscriptomes (Zheludev et al., 2024). Their persistence in the host for months to years and their lack of association with any disease phenotype suggest a symbiotic, rather than pathogenic, relationship. However, their functional role remains one of the most compelling mysteries in modern biology. This article will explore the multi-layered nature of the Obelisk, moving from its concrete molecular definition to its provocative interpretation as a quantum-biological antenna, and situating this within the Enteric Absolute framework—the proposition that the enteric nervous system is the primary neural substrate of consciousness. --- ## 2. Molecular Identity and Functional Characterization The Obelisk's identity is anchored in its molecular and structural features, making it a distinct entity from other RNA-based agents such as viruses and circular RNAs (circRNAs). ### 2.1. A Unique Genetic Entity Unlike viruses, Obelisks lack capsid proteins and show no evidence of inter-human transmission. They are distinct from many circRNAs, which often have regulatory functions or act as templates for protein translation. The key genomic features of an Obelisk include its rod-like helical conformation, measuring ~50 nm in length and 5 nm in diameter, and its coding capacity for 1–2 novel proteins, termed "oblins." These oblins, with a molecular weight of 30–40 kDa, are of particular interest as they share no detectable homology with any known proteins, suggesting they are part of a completely unexplored biological toolkit. Taxonomically, the Obelisk is provisionally placed within the kingdom Riboviria, though its phylum remains unassigned, representing a novel lineage. The proposed genus is Obeliscus, with *Obeliscus hominis* as the proposed type species. Its replication is presumed to be host-dependent, as it does not encode its own viral polymerase. ### 2.2. Genomic Architecture and Diversity The comprehensive bioinformatics study by Hernández-Walías and Reinosa-Fernández (2025) revealed important patterns in Obelisk genetic variability. Analyzing sequences clustered at 90% nucleotide identity, they found: - **High intra-cluster conservation:** Most clusters showed >90% sequence conservation, with the BETA cluster exhibiting 100% conservation and the TAU cluster showing ~95% conservation. - **Significant point mutations:** Despite high overall conservation, many clusters exhibited mutations with frequencies approaching 50%, indicating dynamic evolution within populations. - **Conserved protein domains:** A conserved region in Oblin-1 was identified across all consensus sequences: **RRRGYKDHGSRRFPHEVH**, corresponding to the domain-A region identified by Zheludev et al. (2024). ### 2.3. Evidence of Biological Activity Beyond its genetic blueprint, the Obelisk's biological reality is confirmed through multiple lines of evidence: 1. **Presence and Stability:** Its detection in a wide array of human populations, from urban centers to isolated indigenous groups, points to a global and ancient association. Its stability within a host over extended periods suggests a stable, integrated relationship (Zheludev et al., 2024). 2. **Protein Expression:** The identification of oblin peptides via mass-spectrometry in stool samples and bacterial lysates confirms that these genes are actively transcribed and translated. 3. **Non-Pathogenic Role:** A crucial aspect of its definition is the absence of any correlation with disease. It is found in healthy individuals, marking it as a potential commensal or even a beneficial agent. 4. **Host Association:** The identification of Obelisk-S.s in *Streptococcus sanguinis* SK36 provides a definitive host-pathogen pairing (Zheludev et al., 2024; Maddamsetti & You, 2024). The team of Ivan Zheludev found that their target obelisk in *Streptococcus sanguinis* was nonessential for bacterial survival, with no observed differences in bacterial growth in liquid media with or without the obelisk. These results open questions about the importance, ecology and effect the presence of these RNA entities can have in bacteria. This profile establishes the Obelisk as a biologically active, non-threatening, and globally distributed component of the human gut microbiome. Its exact "unknown role" is what makes the quantum-biological interpretation all the more compelling—particularly when viewed through the lens of the Enteric Absolute, which posits the gut as the primary receiver of consciousness. --- ## 3. The Microbiota–Gut–Brain Axis and Mental Health The gut microbiota and its metabolites play an important role in mental health through the microbiota–gut–brain axis. The composition and abundance of gut microbiota, especially Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, are associated with several mental disorders, such as anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, ASD, and schizophrenia (Xiong et al., 2023). ### 3.1. Anxiety and Depression Patients with anxiety show dramatically decreased microbial richness and diversity. At the phylum level, anxiety patients typically have lower Firmicutes, but higher Bacteroidetes and Fusobacteria. At the genus level, several gut microbiota genera are positively correlated with anxiety, such as *Prevotella*, *Lactobacillales*, *Sellimonas*, *Streptococcus*, and *Enterococcus*. Depression is similarly associated with gut microbiome dysbiosis. Studies show that *Dialister* and *Coprococcus* spp. are decreased in patients with depression, while *Prevotella*, *Klebsiella*, *Streptococcus* and *Clostridium XI* are increased. Fecal transfers from patients with depression to germ-free-like mice can induce depressive-like behaviors, establishing a causal relationship. ### 3.2. Bipolar Disorder and Schizophrenia Bipolar disorder patients show higher abundances of *Clostridiaceae*, *Collinsella*, and the phyla Actinobacteria and Coriobacteria, but lower abundances of *Faecalibacterium* and *Ruminococcaceae*. Schizophrenia patients exhibit reduced abundances of *Ruminococcus* and *Roseburia*, as well as increased *Veillonella* and *Lachnospiraceae*. The gut microbiota can alter neurochemistry and neurologic function via modulating the glutamate–glutamine–GABA cycle. ### 3.3. The Obelisk Connection The Obelisk, as a resident of the gut microbiome, may influence these microbial communities and their metabolites. Its presence or absence could modulate the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), neurotransmitters, and other signaling molecules that affect mental health. The rod-like structure of Obelisks and their resonant properties may allow them to influence microbial communities through mechanisms beyond conventional biochemistry—potentially through quantum-biological signaling. --- ## 4.
