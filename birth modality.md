## Abstract

**Background:** Near-death experiences (NDEs) exhibit consistent phenomenological patterns, yet no systematic framework explains why these patterns vary between individuals. This manuscript proposes the **Enterarchon Perinatal Imprint Hypothesis**: the modality of birth (vaginal vs. cesarean) may contribute to distinct developmental representations that may later influence spatial phenomenology during near-death experiences.

**The Overlooked Variable:** For decades, researchers have attempted to explain NDEs through neurochemistry, brain regions, and psychological factors. Birth modality appears to be insufficiently investigated as a systematic predictor of NDE phenomenology. This manuscript proposes an integrative framework connecting these previously separated domains.

**Method:** Synthesis of peer-reviewed literature from NDE research, developmental neurobiology, and consciousness studies. The framework is presented as a testable hypothesis rather than an established explanation.

**Results:** The literature contains reports of differing NDE spatial phenomenology, including tunnel-like and open-space or surgical-field narratives. Whether these patterns are associated with birth modality has not yet been established systematically.

**Conclusion:** This manuscript proposes that birth modality constitutes a previously underexamined developmental variable that may predict specific spatial features of NDE phenomenology, and proposes the ENS as a candidate biological substrate through which such developmental information could participate in later experience. The Macachor Enterarchon model further hypothesizes that enteric neural circuitry participates in the encoding, transmission, or reconstruction of these representations. This stronger claim remains experimentally unresolved and requires direct testing.

---

## Central Scientific Proposition

> **The Enterarchon Perinatal Imprint Hypothesis does not claim that birth modality has been demonstrated to determine NDE phenomenology. It proposes a falsifiable empirical question: does documented birth modality predict specific spatial characteristics of NDE reports after adjustment for relevant confounders?**

> If an association is demonstrated, subsequent experiments can investigate whether developmental, neural, autonomic, or enteric mechanisms contribute to that relationship. If no association is demonstrated under adequately powered and preregistered conditions, the hypothesis is weakened.

## 1. Introduction

Near-death experiences (NDEs) are profound, subjective experiences that occur when an individual is physiologically or psychologically close to death (Greyson, 1983; van Lommel et al., 2001). NDEs often include:
- A sense of disembodiment (out-of-body experience)
- A tunnel with a bright light
- Encounters with deceased persons or spiritual beings
- A life review
- Feelings of peace and unity

However, not all NDEs are identical. Some experiencers report tunnels; others report surgical-field observations. Some report peace; others report fear. **This variability has remained unexplained.**

## 2. NDE Phenomenology: The Heterogeneity Problem

NDEs are not uniform. The systematic review by Hashemi et al. (2023; n = 465) documented consistent but variable phenomenology. The literature contains reports of differing NDE spatial phenomenology, including tunnel-like and open-space or surgical-field narratives. Whether these patterns are associated with birth modality has not yet been established systematically.

| **NDE Feature** | **Reported In** | **Source** |
|---|---|---|
| Tunnel, directional light, no body sight | Vaginal birth cases | Serdahely & Walker, 1990; Wistrand, 2012 |
| Surgical field, mirror-vision, open space | Cesarean birth cases | Pennachio, 1988; Panditrao et al., 2010; Wistrand, 2012 |

### 2.1 The Enterarchon Interpretation

The hypothesis proposes that the biomechanical and sensory conditions associated with vaginal or cesarean delivery could contribute to distinct developmental representations that may later influence spatial phenomenology.

**This is a hypothesis requiring direct testing.**

## 3. Development of the Enteric Nervous System

### 3.1 Established Biology

Enteric neural development begins early in embryogenesis, with vagal neural crest cells colonizing the developing gut during early embryonic development (Furness, 2006, 2012; Wallace & Burns, 2005). The ENS contains approximately 500 million neurons—more than the spinal cord—organized into independent microcircuits capable of operating without central nervous system input (Furness, 2006).

Furness et al. (2018) characterized the ENS as evolutionarily preceding and independent of the CNS: **"We conclude that an ENS arose before and independently of the CNS. Thus the ENS can be regarded as the first brain."** This is an evolutionary characterization rather than proof that the ENS is the literal first consciousness system.

### 3.2 Hypothesized Perinatal Encoding

The biomechanical and sensory conditions surrounding birth may provide developmental inputs capable of influencing later neural representations. This is a hypothesis, not an established mechanism.

| **Birth Modality** | **Hypothesized Developmental Exposure** | **Hypothesized Spatial Template** |
|---|---|---|
| **Vaginal birth** | Compression through the birth canal | Directional tunnel-gradient |
| **Cesarean birth** | Exposure when the abdominal cavity is opened | Discontinuous open-cavity |

## 5. Coma, Anesthesia, and Preserved Responsiveness

### 5.1 Response to Voices

Numerous clinical studies have documented that comatose patients respond to voices. Auditory processing persists even when the patient is unresponsive. Functional MRI (fMRI) studies have shown that comatose patients' brains activate in response to familiar voices, suggesting that consciousness—or at least perceptual processing—continues.

### 5.2 Response to Heat and Pain

Comatose patients exhibit physiological responses to heat and pain:
- Increased heart rate
- Changes in blood pressure
- Facial grimacing
- Withdrawal reflexes

These responses indicate that the nervous system continues to process sensory input, even when the head-brain is non-functional. **The ENS may participate in these responses, though the precise mechanisms are not fully established.**

### 5.3 Anesthesia Awareness

Anesthesia awareness—the phenomenon in which patients under general anesthesia retain awareness of their surroundings—occurs in approximately 1-2 per 1000 surgeries. Anesthesia awareness demonstrates that clinically administered general anesthesia does not invariably eliminate conscious awareness; it does not establish that awareness occurs independently of brain function.

### 5.4 Consciousness During Cardiac Arrest

The AWARE study investigated recalled awareness during cardiac arrest and resuscitation, providing important evidence concerning the timing and phenomenology of reported consciousness during resuscitation. It does not, by itself, establish that consciousness occurred during a period of complete absence of cerebral activity.

Greyson and Pehlivanova (2025) noted: *"There is a substantial body of empirical evidence ... suggesting that physicalist models fail to account adequately for these common experiences, in which conscious, vivid, and accurate perception has been demonstrated to occur in the absence of normal brain activity."* This remains an area of active investigation.

## 7. Predictions, Falsification Criteria, and Competing Explanations

### 7.1 Formal Prediction

**Prediction P1 — Birth-modality association**

**Primary outcome:** presence/absence of tunnel-like spatial phenomenology.

**Primary predictor:** documented birth modality (vaginal versus cesarean).

**Primary analysis:** multivariable logistic regression estimating the association between birth modality and tunnel phenomenology.

**Secondary outcomes:** open-space phenomenology, perceived movement through a passage, luminous/directional phenomenology, extracorporeal perspective, and surgical-field imagery.

**Sensitivity analyses:** exclude uncertain birth histories; stratify cardiac-arrest and non-cardiac NDEs; separately analyze childhood versus adult NDEs; and test whether anesthesia exposure modifies the association.

**Covariates:** age, sex, cause of NDE, cardiac arrest vs. non-cardiac NDE, anesthesia, medications, hypoxia, cultural background, prior NDE knowledge, childhood trauma, visual/auditory impairment, memory reliability.

### 7.2 Falsification Criterion

> **If birth modality fails to predict NDE spatial phenomenology after preregistered adjustment for relevant confounders, the Perinatal Imprint hypothesis would be substantially weakened.**

### 7.3 Competing Explanations

The predicted association, if observed, would not by itself demonstrate an enteric mechanism. Alternative explanations include:

1. **Cultural expectation:** knowledge of birth narratives could influence retrospective interpretation.
2. **Memory reconstruction:** NDE memories may be reconstructed after the event.
3. **General spatial imagery:** tunnels and open spaces may arise from neurophysiological mechanisms unrelated to birth.
4. **Anesthesia effects:** anesthetic exposure could influence phenomenology.
5. **Hypoxia and hypercarbia:** physiological disturbances may influence visual and spatial experience.
6. **Selection bias:** individuals with unusual NDEs may be more likely to report them.
7. **Recall bias:** individuals may inaccurately report or reconstruct their birth history.
8. **Post-event information:** experiencers may learn about birth circumstances after their NDE.

> **The Enterarchon hypothesis therefore makes a stronger claim only if the birth-modality association survives these competing explanations and the mechanistic contribution of enteric circuitry can subsequently be demonstrated.**

### 7.4 Negative-Control Prediction

> **If the mechanism is specifically related to perinatal spatial encoding, birth modality should show greater association with spatial phenomenology than with phenomenological dimensions having no obvious spatial relationship, such as pleasantness alone.**

This gives a way to distinguish a genuine spatial prediction from a general association between birth history and NDE reporting.

## 8. Proposed Experimental Design

### 8.1 Prospective Registry

A prospective registry could preregister this as the principal endpoint:
- Document birth modality of NDE experiencers
- Document NDE phenomenology using standardized instruments (e.g., Greyson NDE Scale)
- Control for confounders
- Test for association

### 8.2 Retrospective Analysis

Existing NDE databases could be analyzed:
- Extract birth modality from medical records or interviews
- Compare phenomenology between vaginal and cesarean groups
- Control for confounders

### 8.3 Critical Third Group

**Vaginal birth → Cesarean birth → Unknown/undocumented birth modality**

The third category prevents forced classification and allows for missing-data sensitivity analysis.

If sample size permits, distinguish:
- spontaneous vaginal delivery
- assisted vaginal delivery
- planned cesarean
- emergency cesarean

## 10. Limitations

### 10.1 Current Limitations

1. **Retrospective data**: Most NDE studies are retrospective
2. **Self-report**: NDE phenomenology relies on self-report
3. **Memory reliability**: Birth modality may not be accurately recalled
4. **Confounders**: Many variables may influence NDE phenomenology
5. **Small sample sizes**: NDE studies often have limited power

## 12. Conclusion

The Enterarchon Perinatal Imprint Hypothesis proposes that **birth modality may represent a previously underexamined developmental variable in the study of near-death experience phenomenology**.

The central empirical prediction is that documented birth modality will be associated with specific spatial characteristics of subsequently reported NDEs, particularly tunnel-like versus open-space phenomenology, after appropriate adjustment for demographic, clinical, psychological, cultural, and experiential confounders.

The hypothesis further proposes the enteric nervous system as a candidate biological substrate through which perinatal sensory and biomechanical information might participate in later neural representation. This mechanistic proposal remains unresolved.

The **Macachor Enterarchon model** extends this hypothesis by proposing that enteric neural circuitry may participate in the encoding, transmission, or reconstruction of representations associated with NDE phenomenology. The stronger proposition that the ENS constitutes a primary receiver of consciousness, or that consciousness can occur independently of cerebral function, remains an open theoretical claim requiring evidence beyond the observations presently available.

The immediate scientific question is therefore not whether the Enterarchon model has already been demonstrated, but whether its predictions can be subjected to **prospective, preregistered, falsifiable empirical testing**.

The decisive experiment is straightforward in principle:

> **Does documented birth modality predict NDE phenomenology after appropriate control of confounding variables?**

If it does, the finding would establish birth modality as a previously underexamined correlate of NDE phenomenology and justify further investigation of developmental and enteric mechanisms.

If it does not, the Perinatal Imprint Hypothesis would be substantially weakened.

**The Enterarchon Perinatal Imprint is therefore presented as a testable scientific hypothesis—not as an established explanation of consciousness.**
